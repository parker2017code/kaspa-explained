Origin story

Kaspa's fair launch was the path left after the other paths failed

Kaspa's launch structure was not chosen. It was what was left after research into faster proof-of-work ordering ran through DAGLabs and an April 2021 testnet, then through a hardware plan, a presale, and a DeFi pitch, each of which fell apart.

Every earlier structure would have made Kaspa answer to something outside the protocol. Fair launch took the premine, the pre-sale, and the insider table off the starting line before mainnet existed. It proved nothing about later adoption, price, or whether the tech would work.

Research first

The problem came before the coin

Bitcoin at high block rates has one weak point: two honest miners can solve a block seconds apart, and the single-chain rule throws one of those blocks' work away.

Kaspa's research line starts from that exact waste. A blockDAG keeps both blocks visible instead of dropping one, then uses a rule like GHOSTDAG to order that graph into one shared history.

DAGLabs and testnet

DAGLabs came first, then the fair launch

DAGLabs was co-founded by Yonatan Sompolinsky and Guy Corem. It paid for and ran part of the pre-mainnet path: a Golang build of Phantom/GHOSTDAG, then an April 2021 public testnet. There was no token premine, but there was a VC-funded backstory that shaped the hardware, mining, and fair-launch debate before mainnet existed.

Founding date dispute, testnet bundle, and the code's real origin

Public sources disagree on when DAGLabs was founded. An April 26, 2021 Investing.com launch article says 2017, with $8 million raised from Polychain and Accomplice. The HackerNoon interview intro says Sompolinsky founded DAGLabs in 2018.

Guy Corem's April 13, 2021 testnet note calls Kaspa a Golang build of Phantom/GHOSTDAG and a broader form of Nakamoto Consensus. The testnet bundle was a full node, web wallet, faucet, Katnip block explorer, graph inspector, and Kashboard, framed as testnet first and mainnet later, with no pre-mining or founder allocations planned.

Yonatan's HashdagKaspa co-founder Yonatan Sompolinsky's research writing site, where he lays out design ideas ahead of protocol work. archive describes the original Kaspad codebase as built from the Bitcoin client btcd, written in Golang. That holds up; the unsourced "Bitcoin Core 0.19" claim floating around does not.

One comparison from the same interviews is not a failed path and gets read as one. "Solana-like" points at developer and user experience, a single cohesive Rust-friendly toolchain that feels fast to work in. It is not a claim about copying Solana's execution model, which Kaspa L1 does not share.

Launch

Mainnet launched without a premine

Mainnet followed the April 2021 testnet within the year: a fair-launched Proof-of-Work network with no premine, insider allocation, or pre-sales.

The node's own genesis record is the proof, not marketing copy. Rusty-kaspa's genesis.rs ships a checkpoint DAA score and a UTXOUnspent transaction output. Value sits in separate coins, spent whole and replaced by new ones, instead of living in one account balance that gets edited. commitment that trace back to an empty genesis. Mining made the whole supply, but a fair launch didn't guarantee equal access to early mining.

Yonatan's own archive is blunter than the lore: the launch was messy, he baked several bad ideas into the first gamenet rules, and a few thousand CPUs found the project within days, pushing hash rate up no matter what the plan wanted.

The Black Tuesday post puts it in one clause. The gamenet idea never caught air, so he swapped the random block reward for a flat one.

Early stress

Kaspa had to survive real failure

On November 23, 2021, Yonatan published "Kaspa (Black Tuesday)" after a network crash in the 48 hours before. His account: the developers were rebuilding against a known-valid UTXO commitment after a memory problem, not rolling back to a state they liked better.

That is the sourced version of the "two-week bug" story. The genesis rusty-kaspa still ships is dated November 22, 2021, the day before the post, putting the real outage at days rather than weeks.

Two early incidents get told as one. They are separate events with separate fixes, and both repairs are in the code.

Rust, Crescendo, Toccata

Golang was not the final version

Hashdag's 2022 "Kaspa where to" archive says Michael Sutton pushed for a big refactor and a Rust rewrite, because the Golang code carried old debt that made it hard for new coders to work in. How the rewrite was funded is not known here.

The rusty-kaspa repo describes the Rust build as a drop-in replacement for the older Golang node. Its README puts the Crescendo hard fork on May 5, 2025, moving the network from one block a second to ten. The v1.0.0 release notes name the exact trigger, mainnet DAA score 110,165,000.

"Toccata" gets pinned on ten blocks a second often enough to be worth fixing. They are two different forks, a year apart.

1. Read a DAA score

A DAA score rises by about one per accepted block, not once a second: 1 block a second before Crescendo (5 May 2025), 10 after. This converter switches rate exactly at that boundary, marked below.

Crescendo
genesistoday and beyond

Kaspa dates its own changes by this counter, never by the calendar. Converting it is how you check when something actually happened.

Two exact points anchor this: genesis and Toccata's activation. Rates between them come from real elapsed time, not the bare protocol target. Dates after Toccata are an estimate assuming today's rate holds.

A separate demo: catch a miner lying about a block's time

Catch a lying timestamp

A miner who backdates a block can make it look like blocks are arriving slower than they are, and cheapen mining by lowering the difficulty. So every timestamp is checked: it must land after the middle time of the block's last 11 ancestors, and can't sit more than 132 seconds ahead of the checking node's clock.

refused: too old accepted refused: too far ahead

Bitcoin checks time almost the same way

Bitcoin uses the same 11-block median floor, but allows a timestamp up to 2 hours ahead of now. Kaspa allows only 132 seconds: it expects a new block every tenth of a second, not every 10 minutes.

How the date conversion works, and what anchors it

DAA score is a block count, not a timer

Each block inherits its selected parent's DAA score and adds the number of new blocks in its own merge set that qualify for the difficulty window: daa_score = parent_daa_score + (mergeset_size - mergeset_non_daa_count). Source: consensus/src/processes/difficulty.rs, function internal_calc_daa_score, in kaspanet/rusty-kaspa.

Why the old converter was wrong, and what changed

A single flat rate of 10 DAA units per second does not hold all the way back through history, even anchored on Toccata's activation. Kaspa's own consensus config shows that rate only held after Crescendo.

consensus/core/src/config/params.rs sets mainnet's pre_crescendo_target_time_per_block to 1000 milliseconds, one block a second, and its post-Crescendo target_time_per_block to 100 milliseconds, ten a second, with crescendo_activation at DAA score 110,165,000.

A flat 10-per-second rate applied to a pre-Crescendo score overstates elapsed time by roughly ten times for every day, which is many months of error for anything mined before 5 May 2025.

The converter switches rates at that boundary and interpolates between three real anchors: mainnet genesis (DAA 0, an exact protocol constant), Crescendo's activation (DAA 110,165,000, near 5 May 2025 at 15:00 UTC per Kaspa's own announcement), and Toccata's mainnet activation (DAA score 474,165,565, an exact date from the release notes).

The date conversion used above

Genesis anchors the pre-Crescendo segment and Toccata anchors the post-Crescendo segment. Both are exact. The rate within each segment is the real elapsed time between its two anchors divided by the DAA units between them, not the bare protocol target, so the two segments meet with no jump at the boundary.

That interpolation puts the actual average between Crescendo and Toccata about 0.06% off the 10-per-second target across 421 days, which is why the post-Crescendo approximation is called good rather than exact. Dates after Toccata extrapolate forward at the target rate, because there is no later anchor yet.

How the timestamp check works, and where its constants come from

Timestamp rules used above

Past median time: a block's timestamp must be strictly greater than the median of the 11 values closest to the center of a sampled window of its ancestors' timestamps. Source: consensus/src/processes/past_median_time.rs, constant AVERAGE_FRAME_SIZE = 11, implementing KIP-0004, Sparse Difficulty Windows. A timestamp at or before that median fails with RuleError::TimeTooOld, checked in consensus/src/pipeline/header_processor/post_pow_validation.rs.

Future bound: a header is rejected if its timestamp is more than 132 seconds ahead of the validating node's own clock, failing with RuleError::TimeTooFarIntoTheFuture. Source: consensus/src/pipeline/header_processor/pre_ghostdag_validation.rs (max_block_time = unix_now() + timestamp_deviation_tolerance * 1000), constant defined as TIMESTAMP_DEVIATION_TOLERANCE = 132 in consensus/core/src/config/constants.rs.

Why the median is sampled, not walked

Bitcoin's 11 ancestors form a straight line: block 100 always follows block 99. Kaspa's DAG lets many blocks get mined in parallel and then merge together, so there is no single line of 11 to walk.

The median comes from a sampled window across the blocks the network has already folded in, defined by KIP-0004 above. Blocks that arrive at nearly the same moment from different miners can all raise the DAA score together and all count toward that window. None of them is "block 100" on its own.

Bitcoin's rule, checked against its own source

Bitcoin Core rejects a block whose time is at or before GetMedianTimePast(), the median of the 11 preceding blocks (nMedianTimeSpan = 11), with the reject reason time-too-old. It also rejects a block timestamped more than MAX_FUTURE_BLOCK_TIME (2 hours, 7,200 seconds) ahead of the node's own clock, with reject reason time-too-new.

Source: bitcoin/bitcoin, src/chain.h for the two constants and src/validation.cpp for the two checks, near CheckBlockHeader and ContextualCheckBlockHeader. Same shape as Kaspa's rule: a strict median-of-11 floor plus a future ceiling. The ceiling is roughly 55 times looser on Bitcoin, which expects a block every 10 minutes rather than every tenth of a second.
