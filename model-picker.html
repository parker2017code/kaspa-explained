Effort-tier gaps

How a missing tier gets filled

A few rows here needed a figure their source never published at that exact effort tier, most often cost per successful task. Six model families on this board have two or more measured effort settings, real steps from one tier to the next that this page learns from: cost drops about a third a step, response time drops faster than that, and accuracy figures fall 1 to 17 percent depending on which one, never a straight line.

Each missing tier is filled from whichever real row sits closest to it, on its own, never mixed into a real measured row unless marked. Estimates going down the ladder read the curve straight; estimates going up flip it, a weaker guess since a low-effort score can sit on a floor that does not rise back up the same clean way. Every estimated row says which way it came from.

Ranking

How the score works, and where the numbers come from
  1. Three sources, nine dials, and every dial mixes just two benchmarks, split evenly. Artificial Analysis, LiveBench 2026-06-25, and LM Arena. Most figures read 14 August 2026; a few Artificial Analysis figures read 15 August. Seventeen models, one row per effort setting it published: Claude Opus 5 is two rows here, not one, because high effort and max effort are different setups, with different cost and speed.
  2. Each dial's two benchmarks are picked for what they show, not for which source made them. Some dials mix sources (writing code blends a hard Artificial Analysis test with a live LM Arena user vote); some don't (responsiveness is two Artificial Analysis figures, since no other source tracks speed at all). A tenth dial, following instructions, was tried twice and cut both times: its only real second benchmark, IFBench, has real scores for just 5 of the 17 models here, the same thin bar that sank half a dozen other picks. GPQA Diamond stays cut: the top models score 89 to 95 percent on it, too close to rank anyone. Omniscience Index stays cut too: it is built from two benchmarks already scored on their own, so counting it again would count them twice.
  3. Missing one of a dial's benchmarks does not zero the dial. A model scored on some of a dial's benchmarks is blended from what it has, silently, with no per-model caveat cluttering the row. A dial only reads no data for a model when every benchmark behind it is absent for that model.
  4. A model missing too much of what the nine dials draw on is dropped from the roster, not just hidden. Claude Opus 4.8 (High), Claude Opus 4.7 (High), and GPT-5.5 (xHigh) carried no Artificial Analysis data at all, and every one of the nine dials here draws on at least one Artificial Analysis figure, so they are excluded from this roster entirely rather than shown with a partial score.
  5. This page breaks its own "no headline score" rule once, on purpose. LiveBench's own composite and Arena's own Elo rank still feed nothing here. Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index is the one exception: one of two equal legs in the general-quality dial, next to LM Arena's live user picks, because that dial is asking about overall quality, not one single skill.
  6. Each dial is a weight. Turn one up and it takes a bigger share of the score. The shares shown next to the dials add to 100. Only open weights is a filter, because a model either ships its weights or it does not.
  7. A benchmark counts for what its evidence will bear, not for what it claims. The weight written against each figure says how much it should matter, which is an editorial call. Whether the figure can answer at all is measured instead: how much of this roster published it, and how far apart the top five models actually are on it. A thin or saturated benchmark counts for a fraction of a well covered one that still separates the leaders.
  8. Scores are percentiles across the seventeen models here, not raw benchmark values, so a 90 means near the top of this specific field rather than 90 percent correct.
  9. Effort-tier gaps are filled automatically, from the same six-family curve documented under each dial's provenance, never mixed silently into a measured row. Claude Opus 5 (High) shows an estimated cost per successful task this way, anchored off the nearest tier LiveBench actually measured, because LiveBench published only one nearby effort tier for that model family.
  10. Not every model is measured on every factor, and the gap runs one way. A model is scored across the figures it published, which quietly rewards not publishing. A model with at least one published figure among what you asked for is ranked on that; one with none of them is not ranked at all. Any row not measured on everything you asked for says which factors it missed and what share of your question its score actually covers.
  11. Most benchmarks are saturated at the frontier, and each dial says whether it can separate the leaders. A dial ranking a frontier that has already maxed out its benchmarks is ranking noise with a decimal point on it. Each dial carries its own frontier spread, computed from the loaded data, and reads separates, barely separates, or cannot separate.
  12. Cost splits across the two sources that publish it. LiveBench's cost per successful task, because a cheap model that needs three tries is not cheap, and Artificial Analysis's cost per task, what a single try costs whether or not it works. LM Arena publishes no cost data at all.
