How the score works, and where the numbers come from
  1. Three boards, ten dials, twenty-five figures. Artificial Analysis, LiveBench 2026-06-25 and LM Arena, all read 20 August 2026. Thirteen figures come from Artificial Analysis, seven from LiveBench, five from LM Arena. Twenty-two models are ranked, one row per effort setting a model published, because a model at high effort and the same model at max effort are different setups with different price and speed.
  2. A benchmark is here because it still tells the leaders apart, not because it is famous. Every figure was checked on its top-five gap: how far apart the five strongest models are on it. A test the best models have already maxed out cannot rank them. That cut GPQA Diamond, which spans one point across the top five, and with it the AA Intelligence Index, LiveBench reasoning, AA-LCR, Terminal-Bench v2.1, CritPt, MMMU Pro and LiveBench overall. Six of those were live on this page yesterday.
  3. One board here is read and not scored, on purpose. LM Arena's Agent board prints the size of a number without its sign. Whether a model did better or worse than the field shows up only as a small arrow, and the switch from better to worse falls at row 28 of 50. The last row reads 19.82 percent and means minus 19.82. Scoring what is printed would have turned the weakest half of that board into the strongest.
  4. Nothing is counted twice. Each figure feeds exactly one dial. No board's own overall score feeds anything, because those are blends of figures already scored here. Input price is not loaded either: it tracks output price at 0.98, so one price figure does the work of both.
  5. A model needs published scores for at least 14 of the 25 figures to be ranked. That is what takes these three boards from 37 models down to 22. The number 14 is where the data breaks, not a round number picked first: every model with scores from more than one board clears it, and every model with scores from one board only lands on exactly 13. So the floor sorts on whether more than one board has seen the model. Claude Sonnet 5 drops out entirely under that rule, because no Sonnet 5 row clears it.
  6. Missing one of a dial's figures does not zero the dial. A model is scored on whichever of that dial's figures it has. A dial reads no data for a model only when every figure behind it is absent.
  7. Each dial is a weight. Turn one up and it takes a bigger share. The shares next to the dials add to 100. Open weights is the only filter, because a model either ships its weights or it does not.
  8. A figure counts for what its evidence will bear. The weight written against it says how much it should matter, which is a judgment call. Whether it can answer at all is measured: how much of this roster published it, and how far apart the top five are on it. A thin or maxed-out figure counts for a fraction of a well covered one.
  9. Scores are percentiles across these 22 models, not raw benchmark values. A 90 means near the top of this field, not 90 percent correct.
  10. Not every model is measured on everything, and the gap runs one way. A model is scored on what it published, which quietly rewards publishing less. Any row not measured on all of what you asked for says which parts it missed and how much of your question its score covers.
  11. Each dial says whether it can separate the leaders. A dial reading figures the frontier has maxed out is ranking noise with a decimal point on it. Each one carries its own top-five gap, worked out from the loaded data, and reads separates, barely separates, or cannot separate.
  12. Every dial carries two or three figures, never one. A control built on a single number is that number's leaderboard with a slider on it. Seven dials carry three. Three carry two, and each says why in its own panel: the next candidate was measured on too few of these models, or it was a blend of figures already scored here. A third leg that repeats the first two is not more evidence.
