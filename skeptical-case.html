What could go wrong

Kaspa risks and open questions

The case for Kaspa rests on a handful of separate claims, and any one of them can fail without the others failing. If you care about Kaspa, you deserve to know what would break each one.

Main objections

Verification cost

Higher block rates raise the bandwidth, CPU, and storage a node needs to keep up. Push that too far and independent verification concentrates in a handful of well-funded operators.

Security budget

Block rewards decline over time. Unless fees and demand replace that revenue, miner income falls and so does the cost of attacking the network.

External cost

PoW security is bought with electricity, ASICs, facilities, and supply chains. That cost is real and it does not disappear because the chain is fast.

Liquidity

Depth, custody, exchange support, and market-maker coverage do not follow automatically from good architecture. They take years and capital to build.

Mining concentration

ASIC supply is controlled by a small number of manufacturers. Pool concentration and geographic clustering can put practical control in few hands even with an open protocol.

Roadmap execution

Toccata activated on mainnet; vProgs, DAGKnight, and native DeFi are still separate engineering tracks on separate timelines. Treating them as one shipped bundle overstates how much is actually done.

User demand

Low fees and spare capacity are necessary for apps to exist. They are not evidence that anyone will use them twice.

Proof-of-Work external cost

Proof of Work prices Sybil resistance in physical goods: electricity, ASICs, facilities, cooling, supply chains, operators, geography, time. A miner cannot fake identities to gain influence: adding influence means adding hashpower, and hashpower costs money every hour it runs.

That reshapes attack economics. An attacker needs hardware, energy contracts, and sustained operating cost. Capital sitting in a wallet isn't enough. New KAS enters circulation as a reward to miners who already spent those resources, a different issuance model from staking, where new supply and voting weight both trace back to existing token ownership.

What a PoW participant actually controls differs from what a staker controls. A node verifies the chain regardless of who mines it. Mining to your own node lets you help produce blocks, but you're still exposed to the same outside-world risks as every other miner: ASIC supply concentration, cheap-energy access, pool behavior, and regulation.

App ambition can outrun evidence

Toccata shipped. vProgs, DAGKnight, native DeFi, and coordination markets did not ship with it, and a testnet demo of one is not mainnet evidence for the rest, or even for the one it demoed.

What to watch

Count the number of independent explorers, APIs, wallets, and node operators. More of them means less dependence on any single interface going down or going bad. Watch whether roadmap items turn into applications with repeat users outside the teams that built them. A working demo alone doesn't count. Liquidity, custody support, and fee demand will decide whether the thesis holds regardless of how the architecture performs. And watch the language: during a hype cycle, live, testnet, roadmap, and research claims get flattened into one story first.
