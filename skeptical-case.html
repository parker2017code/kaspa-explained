What could go wrong

Kaspa risks and open questions

The case for Kaspa rests on a handful of separate claims, and any one of them can fail without the others failing.

Seven risks, and what would settle each one

Verification cost

Higher block rates raise the bandwidth, CPU, and storage a node needs. Push that too far and only a few well-funded operators can still check the chain.

What would settle it

Many separate operators running nodes, APIs, explorers, and indexers. Fast blocks alone do not settle it.
Security budget

Block rewards decline over time. Unless fees and demand replace that revenue, miner income falls and so does the cost of attacking the network.

What would settle it

Fees and real demand paying for enough hash power over time. Price and enthusiasm alone do not settle it.

Kaspa's block reward is 2.31246515 KAS (api.kaspa.org/info/blockreward): 864,000 blocks a day at 10 blocks per second, 1,997,970 KAS of new supply daily. At $0.02934513 per KAS (CoinGecko, read August 22, 2026), that's about $58,631 a day paid to miners, funding a network running at roughly 311.5 PH/s (api.kaspa.org/info/hashrate). Fees are a rounding error next to that: about 77,207 transactions a day at a median $0.000063 (this site's own chain data) comes to $4.86 a day, roughly 1/12,054th of the subsidy, 0.0083%. The other 99.99% is new supply. Demand pays almost none of it.

The subsidy is also shrinking on its own schedule. Kaspa's reward steps down every month by a factor of 2−1/12, about 5.61%, per rusty-kaspa's coinbase.rs subsidy table. Twelve of those steps compound to exactly half, so holding KAS price flat, the daily payout to miners falls to about $29,315 a year from now and about $3,664 in four years. Fees, unchanged, would need to grow about 6,000-fold in a year and 750-fold in four just to stay this far behind. That is the size of the gap they would have to close before catching up even starts.

Catching up would take real transaction volume. Holding today's $0.000063 median fee flat, matching today's $58,631 subsidy from fees alone needs about 10,771 transactions per second, held continuously. A momentary peak would not be enough. That's roughly 12,000 times Kaspa's own 0.894 tx/s on the same 22 Aug 2026 one-day snapshot this section's other figures use (77,207 daily transactions divided by 86,400 seconds, per CLAIMS.yml's fees_vs_subsidy_2026_08_22 entry), and higher than any one-day sustained rate in this site's own comparison data, including Solana's 1,579.6 on that same one-day basis (see the mining page's traffic-scenarios sourcing for the full derivation; the site's 30-day means, a different and larger figure for a bursty chain like Kaspa, are on chain-comparer). Holding today's transaction count flat instead, the same match needs a median fee near $0.76, about six times Bitcoin's own $0.1317 median. Neither looks close, and both would have to happen while the subsidy itself keeps shrinking underneath them.

This isn't unique to Kaspa: Bitcoin has run the same subsidy-funded deficit for its entire existence and remains the hardest chain to attack. Today, Kaspa's security is bought almost entirely with inflation. People paying to use the chain fund almost none of it, and no visible trend closes that gap on fees alone.

External cost

PoW security is bought with power, ASICs, and supply chains. That cost is real, and a fast chain does not make it go away.
Liquidity

Depth, custody, exchange support, and market makers do not follow from good design. They take years and money.

What would settle it

Users able to get in and out, hold their own keys, keep books, get help, and trade at depth. Holders and volume existing on their own do not settle it.
Mining concentration

A small number of makers control ASICApplication-specific integrated circuit: a chip built to do one job, in Kaspa's case running kHeavyHash, fast. It cannot be reprogrammed for anything else the way a general-purpose CPU or GPU can. supply. Pools cluster, and so does geography, so control can land in few hands even with an open protocol.

Fair-launch context

No premine does not mean no early concentration. The fair-launch comparison plots Kaspa's genesis supply against Ethereum, Cardano, and XRP, and what a fair launch does and doesn't guarantee.
Roadmap execution

Toccata activated on mainnet. vProgs, DAGKnight, and native DeFi are still separate tracks on separate timelines; a testnet demo of one is not evidence for the rest.

What would settle it

A mainnet release and proof it turned on, with wallets, indexers, accepted transactions, and repeat flows. A talk, a branch, or a testnet demo does not settle it.

User demand

Low fees and spare room are needed for apps to exist. They are no proof that anyone will use them twice.

What would settle it

People coming back because an app does the job better on Kaspa than on a normal server. Low fees alone do not settle it.

How many separate node, API, and indexer operators exist. Whether roadmap items turn into apps with repeat users. Staying a demo does not count. And how fast the words live, testnet, roadmap, and research get flattened into one story in a hype cycle.
