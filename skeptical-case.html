What could go wrong

Kaspa risks and open questions

The case for Kaspa rests on a handful of separate claims, and any one of them can fail without the others failing. Here is what would break each one.

Short answer

Node and indexer pressure, a security budget that needs fees to eventually replace block rewards, the real external cost of proof-of-work hardware and power, thin liquidity, mining concentration, and an app roadmap still years from proven repeat use: those are the load-bearing risks.

Toccata activated on mainnet; vProgs, DAGKnight, and native DeFi did not activate with it, and a testnet demo of one is not evidence for the rest.

The seven objections, one by one

Verification cost

Higher block rates raise the bandwidth, CPU, and storage a node needs. Push that too far and only a few well-funded operators can still check the chain.

Security budget

Block rewards decline over time. Unless fees and demand replace that revenue, miner income falls and so does the cost of attacking the network.

External cost

PoW security is bought with power, ASICs, and supply chains. That cost is real, and a fast chain does not make it go away.

Liquidity

Depth, custody, exchange support, and market makers do not follow from good design. They take years and money.

Mining concentration

A small number of makers control ASIC supply. Pools cluster, and so does geography, so control can land in few hands even with an open protocol.

Roadmap execution

Toccata activated on mainnet. vProgs, DAGKnight, and native DeFi are still separate tracks on separate timelines. Treating them as one shipped bundle overstates how much is done.

User demand

Low fees and spare room are needed for apps to exist. They are no proof that anyone will use them twice.

No premine does not mean no early concentration. The fair-launch comparison plots Kaspa's genesis supply against Ethereum, Cardano, and XRP, and what a fair launch does and doesn't guarantee.

Proof-of-work's external cost

Proof of work buys its Sybil resistance with real goods: power, ASICs, buildings, cooling, supply chains, time. A miner cannot fake identities to gain a bigger say. More say means more hash power, and hash power costs money every hour it runs.

That changes the math on attacks. An attacker needs hardware, power deals, and a bill that runs every day. Money in a wallet is not enough. New KAS goes to miners who already spent those things. Staking sends new supply and votes back to whoever already holds tokens.

A node checks the chain no matter who mines it. Mining to your own node lets you help make blocks, and you still carry the same outside risks as any other miner: ASIC supply, cheap power, pool behavior, and regulators.

The attack cost calculator prices a 51% attack in dollars per hour against current hash rate and hardware cost, next to other chains for scale.

What pays for the hash rate

Kaspa's block reward is 2.31246515 KAS (api.kaspa.org/info/blockreward). At 10 blocks per second that's 864,000 blocks a day, so 1,997,970 KAS of new supply a day. At $0.02934513 per KAS (CoinGecko, fetched live), that's about $58,631 a day paid to miners, funding a network running at roughly 311.5 PH/s (api.kaspa.org/info/hashrate).

Fee revenue is a rounding error next to that. Kaspa runs about 77,207 transactions a day at a median fee of $0.000063 (both from this site's own chain data), which is 77,207 × $0.000063, about $4.86 a day. Divide the two: $58,631 ÷ $4.86 is roughly 12,054. Fees fund about 0.0083% of what miners are actually paid. The other 99.99% is new supply, not demand.

The subsidy is also shrinking on its own schedule. Kaspa's reward steps down every month by a factor of 2−1/12, about 5.61%, per rusty-kaspa's coinbase.rs subsidy table. Twelve of those steps compound to exactly half, so holding KAS price flat, the daily payout to miners falls to about $29,315 a year from now and about $3,664 in four years. Fees would still be running at roughly $4.86 a day if nothing else changed, meaning they'd need to grow about 6,000-fold in a year and 750-fold in four just to stay this far behind, not to catch up.

What would catching up actually take? Holding today's $0.000063 median fee flat, matching today's $58,631 subsidy from fees alone needs about 10,771 transactions per second, sustained, not peak. That's roughly 12,000 times Kaspa's own measured 0.895 TPS, and higher than any sustained rate in this site's own comparison data, including Solana's 1,175. Holding today's transaction count flat instead, the same match needs a median fee near $0.76, about six times Bitcoin's own $0.1317 median. Neither looks close, and both would have to happen while the subsidy itself keeps shrinking underneath them.

None of that is unique to Kaspa. Bitcoin has run this exact deficit, subsidy funding almost all of miner income, for its entire existence, and it is still the most attacked-resistant chain that exists. A security budget paid mostly by new supply is normal for a young network, not a special defect. Price appreciation alone can offset a shrinking subsidy for a long time, and a falling budget does not switch a network off: it lowers the hash rate that stays profitable to run, and the real question is whether what's left still makes an attack uneconomic against the value it's protecting, not whether the subsidy hits zero. What this arithmetic does establish is narrower and still true: today, right now, Kaspa's security is bought almost entirely with inflation, not with people paying to use the chain, and no visible trend closes that gap on fees alone.

Where app-roadmap talk outruns the evidence

Toccata shipped. vProgs, DAGKnight, native DeFi, and coordination markets did not ship with it. A testnet demo of one is not mainnet proof for the rest, or even for the one it demoed.

What to watch

Count the separate explorers, APIs, wallets, and node operators. The more there are, the less rides on any one going down or going bad. Watch whether roadmap items turn into apps with repeat users outside the teams that built them. A working demo alone doesn't count. Liquidity, custody support, and fee demand will settle whether the thesis holds, whatever the design does. And watch the words: in a hype cycle, live, testnet, roadmap, and research claims get flattened into one story first.
