Crypto from zero

Crypto starts with shared records

Your bank balance, your concert ticket, your game items: all of them are records. Storing a record is easy. The hard part is deciding who can change it, who checks the change, and why everyone else has to accept the result.

Crypto keeps one shared digital record without one trusted operator. Remove the operator and you inherit their old job: keys, consensus, incentives, markets, and group action all have to be rebuilt from scratch.

The starting problem

Why digital ownership is hard

Physical cash has location

Hand someone a banknote and you no longer hold it. The physical object cannot sit in two wallets at once: scarcity is a property of matter, free of charge.

Digital data copies for free

Copy a file, edit a database row, screenshot an image: nothing stops you. Scarcity doesn't survive the jump to bits, so digital money has to manufacture it with rules instead of physics.

Summary: Coins never actually move anywhere. A valid transaction changes an accepted record, and that record is the whole asset.

Trusted operator

Why a bank database is simpler

Most systems solve the ownership problem with an operator. A bank, exchange, payment app, or platform runs the database and decides which updates count. One authority fixing mistakes, blocking fraud, reversing payments, and pushing upgrades is a cheaper design than the alternative, right up until you need something the authority won't give you.

Operator modelWhat it gives youWhat it can cost

Why cryptography

Crypto needs proof without a login desk

Private key

The secret that controls spending authority. If someone steals yours, the network can't tell the thief from you: there is no ID check behind the math.

Public key or address

Lets anyone verify a signature is valid without ever seeing the private key that produced it.

Signature

Proof that the key holder authorized this specific transaction. It stands in for the username, password, and account admin a normal platform would use.

Hash

A fingerprint for data. Change one byte of the input and the fingerprint changes completely, which is what lets a block chain to its history and makes tampering visible.

What breaks without keys: the system falls back on a central login authority or an identity registry to decide who can move a balance.

Transactions and blocks

Why transactions and blocks exist

Transaction

A signed instruction: spend this value, create that new value, pay this fee. Every field in it exists so a stranger's node can check it without asking the sender anything else.

Why it exists: the network needs one precise, self-contained object a stranger's node can validate alone.

Block

A batch of valid transactions linked to prior history. Nodes either accept the whole batch under the protocol's rules or reject it. There is no partial credit.

Why it exists: the network needs a shared unit of history that a majority can agree happened.

Consensus

Why records need agreement

A record is only worth something if strangers agree it's the record. Show Bob one ledger and Carol another and you've just spent the same coin twice. Consensus exists because thousands of independent computers see network messages arrive in different orders, and they still have to converge on one shared state anyway.

Which transaction came first? Which block counts? Was the same value spent twice? Did a miner or validator follow the rules? Can history be rewritten cheaply? Can users verify the supply?

Mining and staking

Consensus has to cost something

Proof of Work

PoW prices out fake history with a cost outside the system: hardware, electricity, time. Miners propose blocks and get paid only if that work sticks in accepted history.

Tradeoff: energy use, ASICs, mining pools, industrial concentration pressure.

Proof of Stake

PoS prices out fake history with a cost inside the system: validators post the native asset as collateral and can lose it for provable bad behavior, depending on the design.

Tradeoff: wealth concentration, delegation, staking-provider power, harder social-recovery questions when things go wrong.

Tokens

Why public networks often use tokens

A permissionless network needs some internal economic unit to price fees, pay rewards, resist spam, and fund its own security. Wanting a token is not the same as needing one.

Object tracked

The ledger records ownership of the asset itself.

Fee asset

Users pay for scarce block space and avoid free spam.

Security reward

Miners or validators are paid to operate and defend the system.

Market entry

Open markets let users, miners, validators, apps, and investors enter or exit without one issuer approving every participant.

Simple check: most tokens don't need to exist. Ask what this one does that BTC, ETH, USDC, or a plain database doesn't already do.

UTXO and account models

Two ways to track state

Account model

A balance table. Alice has 10, sends 3, and now Alice has 7 while Bob has 3. Intuitive, and the default for smart-contract platforms, until two transactions touch the same shared balance at once and ordering starts to matter.

UTXO model

More like cash notes than a ledger. Alice spends a 10-coin output, Bob receives a 3-coin output, Alice receives a 7-coin change output, and the original 10-coin note is gone for good.

Kaspa uses a UTXO model, same as Bitcoin. That single choice shapes how validation works, how much can run in parallel, and what shape future programmability can even take.

How to read it: the old output is consumed once. New outputs carry the next spendable state.

Step 8

Where Kaspa enters

Bitcoin-style chains pick one block path at a time and throw away the rest. That's conservative and it works, but it caps how fast blocks can arrive: two honest miners who solve a block seconds apart end up competing, and only one of them counts. Kaspa's bet is that a mined UTXO network can keep both blocks, order them with GHOSTDAG, and get payments closer to real time without changing what PoW is securing.
