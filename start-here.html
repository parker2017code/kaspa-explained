Start with the problem

Every crypto design answers one question: what breaks when you take away the single trusted operator?

A bank holds your balance, checks each change, and cleans up the mess when something goes wrong. Take the bank out and that work does not vanish. Keys, agreement rules, rewards, and open markets are what grows back in its place, and each one arrives with a bill.
What "grows back" means, one line each

Keys stand in for a login: whoever holds the private key can move the balance, and nobody else can. Agreement rules replace the bank's internal ledger check: strangers running independent software still have to land on one shared history. Rewards replace payroll: something has to pay for the work of proposing and checking blocks. Open markets replace a teller window: getting a balance in or out means finding another party to trade with. No issuer stands behind it.

The starting problem

Why owning something digital is hard

A banknote cannot sit in two pockets at once, so scarcity comes free with the paper. A file copies for nothing, so digital money has to build scarcity back out of rules. Most systems do that by naming an operator: a bank or an exchange that runs the database and can freeze, reverse, or gate what you hold. A public crypto network replaces that operator with rules any participant can check alone.

What each option actually trades away

A bank or payment app gives support, reversals, and fraud handling, and costs you account freezes, operating hours, and jurisdiction limits. An exchange gives fast internal balances and an easy sign-up, and costs you custody risk and withdrawal risk. A public network gives rules nobody can bend for you personally, and costs you more moving parts and mistakes nobody can undo.

Summary: coins never move anywhere. A valid transaction changes an accepted record, and that record is the whole asset.

Proof without a login desk

Keys take over from the account

No operator means no password reset, so math stands in for both. A private key is the secret that authorizes a spend; a signature proves the key holder approved this exact transaction, the job a username and password do elsewhere. A hash is a fingerprint for data: change one byte and it changes completely, which is how a block commits to the history behind it. A block batches signed transactions against that history, and a node takes the whole batch or none of it.

Getting there also takes agreement: thousands of independent computers see messages land in different orders and still have to settle on one shared history, ruling out theft, double-spends, and rewritten history along the way. Then it takes a cost, or fake history is free to fake. How Kaspa compares lays out Proof of Work against Proof of Stake choice by choice; Kaspa pays the Proof of Work bill.

Simple check: most tokens do not need to exist. Ask what this one does that BTC, ETH, USDC, or a plain database does not already do.

Two ways to track state

Balances or notes

Account model

A balance table. Alice has 10, sends 3, and now Alice has 7 while Bob has 3. It reads well, and it is the default for smart-contract platforms, until two transactions touch the same balance at once and order starts to matter.

UTXO model

Closer to cash notes than to a table. Alice spends a 10-coin output, Bob receives a 3-coin output, Alice receives a 7-coin change output, and the old 10-coin note is gone for good.

Kaspa uses the UTXO model, same as Bitcoin. That one choice shapes how a node checks a transaction, how much work can run side by side, and what shape later programmability can take. Spend the wallet below and watch a UTXO get consumed once, whole, and replaced by new outputs.

Where Kaspa enters

Keep the mining, fix the ordering

Bitcoin-style chains pick one block path at a time and throw the rest away. That caps how fast blocks can arrive: two honest miners who solve a block seconds apart end up competing for only one slot. Kaspa's bet is that a mined UTXO network can keep both blocks, order them with GHOSTDAGThe rule that ranks and orders every block a miner produces, kept ones included, instead of discarding all but one., and bring payments closer to real time without changing what the mining secures. How Kaspa compares works that through against Bitcoin, Ethereum, and Solana.

Quick corrections

Four things beginners get backwards

A low unit price usually just means more supply

Market cap, float, and real demand carry more signal than the price of one unit.

Speed splits into separate stages

A network can be quick at inclusion and slow at settlement. Confirmation depth, throughput, and how a wallet feels are measured apart.

Influence piles up in more than one place

Mining is the obvious one. Nodes, exchanges, wallets, and RPC providers each form a choke point of their own.

Crypto is several markets sharing one ticker list

Stablecoins, meme coins, governance tokens, and mined money price on completely different things.
