Crypto from scratch

What crypto does, and what it's worth

Crypto earns its cost when strangers need one shared record of who owns what, and no single firm, bank, or state is trusted to keep it. Every other question, including price, comes back to that one job.

The primitive

A blockchain is a costly shared ledger

One list of who owns what, kept by thousands of strangers at once, with no company, bank, or state holding the copy that counts. Every holder checks every new entry against the same rules, and changing an old entry means redoing the work behind everything after it.

That checking is the cost. Where the strangers really do need a record none of them controls, nothing cheaper does the job. Where one of them could simply run the database, a blockchain is a slower and more expensive way to reach the same list.

NIST's definition, and why it's narrower than the marketing one

NIST IR 8202 defines a blockchain by four properties: hard to alter, tamper-evident, replicated across many holders, and typically leaderless. Nothing in that definition requires a token, a mining reward, or decentralization beyond copy count. Bitcoin, stablecoins, NFTs, DeFi, and smart contracts are all apps layered on that one narrow structure, not defined by it.

Nobody can push an update to it either. Each operator running the software picks which rules to enforce, and a fork is what happens when they stop picking the same ones.

Soft forks, hard forks, and the bets other projects made

Soft-fork changes tighten the rules, so upgraded nodes enforce new limits while un-upgraded nodes still see those blocks as valid. Hard-fork changes break what old software accepts, sometimes planned and boring, sometimes the moment a chain splits in two.

Bitcoin Cash split over block size. Ethereum traded Bitcoin's spare rule set for programmable state. Solana-style chains bought speed with hardware most people can't run. Monero and Zcash bought privacy with heavier math and legal heat, and stablecoins bet on an issuer or a collateral pool over a central bank.

Where crypto fits

Where it wins, and where it loses

Four places it does real work

Bearer digital assetsWhoever holds the private key controls the asset; an exchange freeze cannot touch a self-held coin.
Cross-border settlementStablecoins are flawed dollar tokens, still one of crypto's clearest wins: paid abroad in minutes, not days.
Programmable escrowSmart contracts hold up when the test is plain: both sides signed, a vesting date arrived.
Open-network incentivesTokens pay the validators and storage hosts an open network runs on, with no one firm hiring all of them.

Four places it usually loses

Payments at homeCards, Pix, UPI, SEPA, and Apple Pay beat crypto on refunds, fraud handling, and support.
Ordinary databasesIf one owner already controls the users, data, and rules, a blockchain only adds cost to a solved problem.
Anything a court settlesFraud, a wrong-address payment, a divorce, a stolen name: cases courts exist for, and code cannot reach them.
Facts from the physical worldA blockchain keeps whatever gets typed into it. Lie about where the tuna came from and the chain keeps the lie forever.

"Trustless" is the wrong word. Crypto moves trust around: what you took from the bank teller lands on code, wallets, bridges, oracles, and law.

Market value

Why a coin has a price, and what the number means

A token price is smoke; the question is what's burning underneath it. Four things push it: someone needs the asset to pay fees, settle a trade, stake, or post collateral; scarcity people believe in; higher rewards buying more security; and reflexivity, price rising because people expect it to.

Market cap is not company value

Market cap is price times circulating supply, nothing more. That much cash never had to flow in, no revenue sits behind the number, and nobody could sell the whole supply at that price. One KAS costs less than one BTC simply because there are far more of them; compare market cap and liquidity instead of unit price.

More numbers that mislead, and what open markets help and break

Price per coin means nothing without supply attached, circulating supply can differ sharply from total supply, and a low float inflates paper valuations without matching real capital. Volume can mean liquidity, hype, wash trading, or panic. Fully diluted value shows the dilution still coming, and social dominance measures attention, not uptake.

Markets help: miners and validators can sell rewards, apps can price collateral, and anyone can enter or exit without asking one issuer. Markets break: pump-and-dumps run easy, insiders use retail liquidity as exit liquidity, and leverage forces violent selling.

Early buyers profiting in an open market does not make an asset a Ponzi. What does: a fixed promised return, yield paid from new deposits instead of income, or hidden insiders.

Bridge to Kaspa

Judge Kaspa where shared records need neutrality

Kaspa's lane is narrow: proof of work, self-custody, global settlement, censorship resistance, fast confirmations, and later, apps that prove their own rules.

Four mechanisms carry it. A blockDAG orders parallel blocks instead of making them race for one slot. The UTXO model settles who owns what. Mass rules price the compute and storage a transaction costs, not just its byte size. Covenants let a coin carry rules about how it can be spent next.
