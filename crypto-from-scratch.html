Crypto from scratch

What crypto does, and what it's worth

Crypto earns its cost when strangers need one shared record of who owns what, and no single firm, bank, or state is trusted to keep it. Every other question, including price, comes back to that one job.

The primitive

A blockchain is a costly shared ledger

Where that agreement is real, crypto does work nothing cheaper does. Where it isn't, a blockchain is a slow, costly database.

NIST's definition, and what's built on it

NIST's definition is the plain one: a digital ledger that is hard to change and that shows any change, kept in many copies instead of one place, and usually with nobody in charge. Bitcoin, stablecoins, NFTs, DeFi, and smart contracts are all apps built on that one narrow idea.

Crypto has no company update lever: each side picks up new rules on its own, and a fork is what happens when they stop agreeing.

Soft forks vs. hard forks, and five chains' bets

Soft-fork changes tighten the rules, so upgraded nodes enforce new limits while un-upgraded nodes still see those blocks as valid. Hard-fork changes break what old software accepts, sometimes planned and boring, sometimes the moment a chain splits in two. Bitcoin Cash split over block size. Ethereum traded Bitcoin's spare rule set for programmable state. Solana-style chains bought speed with hardware most people can't run, Monero and Zcash bought privacy with heavier math and legal heat, and stablecoins bet on an issuer or a collateral pool over a central bank. Kaspa kept PoW and UTXO and swapped the single chain for a blockDAG. Kaspa's fair launch wasn't the plan from day one; it's what was left after DAGLabs, an April 2021 testnet, and several hardware, presale, and business-model paths that fell through. Where crypto fits

Six places it does real work

The six, with the mechanism for each
Bearer digital assetsWhoever holds the private key controls the asset; an exchange freeze cannot touch a self-held coin.
Cross-border settlementStablecoins are flawed dollar tokens, still one of crypto's clearest wins: paid abroad in minutes, not days.
Collateral and on-chain marketsThe legal wrapper still decides what the asset is in court; a token adds ownership a program can move any hour.
Programmable escrowSmart contracts hold up when the test is plain: both sides signed, a vesting date arrived.
Digital provenanceCopyright and legal title stay off-chain. An NFT is good at one job: the record of who owned the thing.
Open-network incentivesTokens pay the validators and storage hosts an open network runs on, with no one firm hiring all of them.

Bad fit

Six places it usually loses

The six, with the mechanism for each
Payments at homeCards, Pix, UPI, SEPA, and Apple Pay beat crypto on refunds, fraud handling, and support.
Ordinary databasesIf one owner already controls the users, data, and rules, a blockchain only adds cost to a solved problem.
Anything a court settlesFraud, a wrong-address payment, a divorce, a stolen name: cases courts exist for, and code cannot reach them.
Private recordsPublic ledgers hide names, not history; tie one address to a person and every payment it made is on view.
Facts from the physical worldA blockchain keeps whatever gets typed into it. Lie about where the tuna came from and the chain keeps the lie forever.
Credit and wrapped real assetsNormal lending runs on who you are and what a court can do about it; wrapping a house in a token leaves who owns and taxes it untouched.

Tradeoffs

What you get and what it costs

"Trustless" is the wrong word. Crypto moves trust around: what you took from the bank teller lands on code, wallets, bridges, oracles, and law. It shrinks the list of people you have to trust, and lets you check the ones left.

Market value

Why a coin has a price at all

A token price is smoke; the question is what's burning underneath it. Four things push it: someone needs the asset to pay fees, settle a trade, stake, or post collateral; scarcity people believe in; higher rewards buying more security; and reflexivity, price rising because people expect it to.

The five roles a token can play, and the beginner trap in each
RoleWhat it doesBeginner warning
Thing being trackedThe ledger defines and transfers the asset itself.A token can exist without being useful outside speculation.
FeesUsers pay for scarce block space and spam resistance.Low fees help UX but can weaken fee revenue or invite spam.
Security budgetMiners or validators need paying to defend the record.Rewards may come from inflation, fees, or both.
CollateralSome systems require staked tokens or bonded assets.Stake-based security pools around big holders and custodians.
GovernanceSome tokens vote on parameters, treasuries, or upgrades.Governance rights can be weak, captured, or mostly symbolic.

Rule: a price pump doesn't prove uptake, and a high market cap doesn't prove usefulness. A solid project can still be overpriced.

The number everyone quotes

Market cap is not company value

Market cap is price times circulating supply, nothing more. That much cash never had to flow in, no revenue sits behind the number, and nobody could sell the whole supply at that price.

Five more numbers that mislead, and what open markets help and break

Price per coin means nothing without supply attached, circulating supply can differ sharply from total supply, and a low float inflates paper valuations without matching real capital. Volume can mean liquidity, hype, wash trading, or panic. Fully diluted value shows the dilution still coming, and social dominance measures attention, not uptake.

Markets help: miners and validators can sell rewards, apps can price collateral, and anyone can enter or exit without asking one issuer. Markets break: pump-and-dumps run easy, insiders use retail liquidity as exit liquidity, and leverage forces violent selling.

Kaspa/BTC example: one KAS costs less than one BTC simply because there are far more of them. Compare market cap and liquidity instead of unit price.

Early buyers profiting in an open market does not make an asset a Ponzi. What does: a fixed promised return, yield paid from new deposits instead of income, or hidden insiders.

Launch design

Initial ownership shapes trust

A fair launch removes the official allocation table, not the funding and early-miner questions a premine would have paid for. Kaspa mined its supply from zero: see that shape plotted against three chains that started with a premine or a sale.

Five launch models, what each buys and risks
Launch modelWhat it improvesWhat it risks
Fair launch / mined launchLower official insider-allocation risk.No built-in treasury, early-miner advantage, harder funding.
PremineFunds development, legal work, grants, audits, and integrations.Insider control, sell pressure, trust problems.
Public sale or ICOFast fundraising and broad early buyer base.Legal risk, hype buying, sloppy product work.
VC allocationReal funding and business support.Unlock overhang and retail exit-liquidity risk.
AirdropRewards users and spreads supply wider.Farming, sybil attacks, instant sell pressure.

Users care about payments, custodyWho actually holds the private keys: you, an exchange, or a custodian, decides who can move the funds., privacy, and fees; traders watch liquidity and the story; miners watch rewards and difficulty; developers want tooling, grants, and stable rules; regulators watch tax, AML, and consumer risk.

Decision rule

Crypto makes sense when at least three are true

Fewer than three, and the project is bolting crypto onto a problem that never needed it. The hardest check: if the job is proving an off-chain fact is true, no ledger can do that for you.

Bridge to Kaspa

Judge Kaspa where shared records need neutrality

Kaspa's lane is narrow: proof of work, self-custody, global settlement, censorship resistance, fast confirmations, and later, apps that prove their own rules. Its blockDAG, UTXO model, mass rules, and covenants are its answers to ordering, double-spends, state cost, and finality.
